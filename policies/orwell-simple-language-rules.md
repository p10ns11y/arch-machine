### Version One: Deliberately Bad Explanation (designed to irk Orwell)

In the hallowed and august annals of linguistic and rhetorical discourse, one finds oneself inexorably compelled to observe that the insidious and multifarious deterioration of the English tongue, particularly within the murky and oftentimes obfuscated realms of political oratory, ideological exposition, and contemporary prose composition, constitutes nothing less than a veritable cancer upon the very fabric of clear and coherent thought itself. It is a matter of no small moment that the habitual utilization of stale metaphors, pretentious diction, foreign phrases, and a plethora of utterly meaningless words serves, in a most unfortunate and regrettable manner, to obfuscate rather than elucidate, thereby perpetuating a vicious and self-reinforcing cycle wherein sloppy language begets sloppy thinking, and sloppy thinking in its turn engenders yet further linguistic degeneration of the most lamentable kind. One cannot help but be struck by the profound necessity of adhering to certain foundational principles which, though they may at first glance appear somewhat pedestrian and lacking in intellectual sophistication, are in point of fact indispensable to the restoration of intellectual integrity and the revitalization of the noble art of prose composition. These six rules, as it were, represent the sine qua non of any serious attempt to revitalize the noble art of prose composition. Yet it must be conceded, with a certain degree of philosophical resignation and a heavy heart, that there exist occasions when the exigencies of the situation demand that one break these very rules rather than commit an act of outright barbarism upon the sensibilities of the discerning reader.

### Version Two: Clean, Simplified Version (follows Orwell’s rules)

Bad language makes thought unclear.  
Unclear thought leads to more bad language.  
This cycle is especially bad in politics.

Orwell gives six rules to fix it:

1. Never use a metaphor or simile you see in print.
2. Use a short word instead of a long one.
3. Cut out any word you can.
4. Use the active voice, not the passive.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, scientific word, or jargon if you can find an everyday English word.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say something outright barbarous.

The main point is simple: think clearly. Write clearly. Do not hide ugly ideas behind vague words.
